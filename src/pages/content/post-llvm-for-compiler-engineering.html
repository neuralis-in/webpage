Why we bet on LLVM for compiler engineering.

Writing a compiler from scratch sounds romantic. In practice it's six months of redoing what LLVM solved in 2003. Here's the field-tested case for IR-first toolchains, DSLs, and accelerator targets.

Pranav Chiku 02 May 2026 8 min read

When a client asks for a custom compiler — for a new ISA, a domain-specific language, a hardware accelerator — the first question we ask is: why aren't you using LLVM?

The reflexive answers are familiar. "LLVM is too heavy." "We want full control." "Our language is special." All of them are reasonable in isolation. Almost none survive eighteen months of production load.

The build-it-yourself trap

Writing a compiler from scratch sounds like a clean engineering exercise. Lex, parse, type-check, lower, optimize, codegen. Each stage is a well-defined pipeline. Each is a research field.

The trap is that the parts you actually care about — the domain semantics, the optimizations specific to your workload, the calling convention to your accelerator — are a small fraction of the work. The rest is undifferentiated compiler plumbing that LLVM solved twenty years ago.

We've audited three from-scratch compilers. All three were spending 80% of their engineering bandwidth re-implementing well-understood algorithms: dominator trees, SSA construction, register allocation, instruction scheduling. None of that work was differentiating their product.

What LLVM actually gives you

LLVM is not a compiler. It's a tower of compilers, presented as a library. Most of its value is the contract the IR represents — a target-independent, SSA-form representation that lets you plug your work in at exactly the level it belongs.

  • The IR is the contract. If you can lower your DSL to LLVM IR, you inherit every optimization pass — inlining, dead code elimination, vectorization, loop unrolling, alias analysis — for free.
  • The backends ship target code. 30+ supported targets, with everything from x86-64 to RISC-V to GPU IRs. New hardware? You write a backend, not a compiler.
  • The pass infrastructure composes. Custom passes layer into the standard pipeline. Your domain-specific peephole patterns, alias analyses, and vectorization hints run alongside the standard ones.

This is the leverage. You write the parts that are unique to your domain. LLVM does the rest.

Frontend, IR, or backend — pick your fight

"Use LLVM" isn't one decision. It's three, and they live at different layers of the stack. Knowing which layer your problem belongs at saves months.

If you're building a new language

You need a frontend. Parse to AST, type-check, lower to LLVM IR. Reuse the optimization pipeline and the backends entirely.

The frontend is where domain semantics live. It's also where most compiler bugs come from. Spend your engineering budget here.

If you're targeting new hardware

You need a backend. Define your target's instruction set, register file, calling convention, ABI. Inherit the language ecosystem — every LLVM-targeting compiler can now produce code for your hardware.

Backends are intricate. The TableGen DSL has a learning curve. Plan for that. But the payoff — every existing language compiling to your accelerator — is enormous.

If you're extracting performance

You need custom passes. Profile-guided. Domain-specific. Layered into the existing pipeline.

We've shipped custom passes that delivered 4-10x speedups on specific workloads — typically by encoding domain invariants (alignment, alias freedom, value ranges) that the generic LLVM passes can't infer.

Pitfalls you should know about

LLVM is mature, but it's not without sharp edges. The things that bite us most often:

  • The IR is verbose. Lowering to it cleanly takes more taste than the documentation suggests. Bad IR layout will defeat optimization passes that look fine in isolation.
  • Passes assume IR invariants. Break them — for example, by emitting unreachable code with live values, or by failing to insert llvm.assume intrinsics — and you'll get crashes deep in optimization with stack traces that look nothing like your code.
  • Pass ordering matters. Performance comes from the right sequence, not adding more. The default pipeline (-O3) is the product of years of tuning. Custom pipelines need empirical validation.
  • Debugging is its own skill. Master -print-after-all, opt-bisect-limit, and the various dump modes. Without them, debugging an optimization pass is close to impossible.

When LLVM is the wrong tool

Sometimes the answer is genuinely not LLVM. A few cases where we've recommended against it:

  • Your DSL is small. SQL fragments, templating mini-languages, query filters. Embed them into a host language. Don't compile.
  • Your target is purely interpreted. Lua-style scripting, configuration languages, simple rule engines. A tree-walking or bytecode interpreter is faster to build and easier to debug.
  • You need source-level transformations. Refactoring tools, linters, codemods. Look at Tree-sitter, libCST, Roslyn, or LSIF tooling. LLVM operates below the source level — it's the wrong layer.
  • You want to ship in a browser. LLVM bundles are heavy. WASM-targeting tools that don't drag in the full pipeline (Cranelift, Wasmtime) are usually a better fit.

The wager, summarized

LLVM is right when you need performance, target portability, or sophisticated optimization. Outside that triangle, simpler tools win.

The bet we make at Neuralis is that for production compiler work — the kind that has to outlive the team that wrote it, run on hardware that doesn't exist yet, or extract single-digit-percent improvements at scale — LLVM is almost always the right substrate.

What we own is the IR-level reasoning, the domain-specific passes, the lowering strategy. What LLVM owns is everything that's been solved. That division of labor is the entire point.
