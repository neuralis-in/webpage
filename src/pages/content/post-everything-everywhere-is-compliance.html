Everything, everywhere is compliance.

For two decades the response to rising regulation was to hire another person. That equation is finally breaking. What it actually takes to put agents into regulated workflows — and why a 90% accurate compliance product is still 100% wrong.

The Neuralis Team 28 May 2026 10 min read

Over the last twenty years the fastest-growing occupation in the US was manicurists. Running close behind, and almost nobody outside the industry noticed, was compliance officers. There are now more than 400,000 of them in the United States, with 33,000+ new openings projected annually. Title 12 of the Code of Federal Regulations, which governs banking, grew more between 2010 and 2014 than it had in the prior thirty years combined.

The corporate response to that growth has been almost comically simple: hire another person. And then another. And then another. In 2024, TD Bank was fined $3 billion for failing to monitor 92% of its transactions, sitting on a backlog of 70,000 detection alerts that started in 2018. The pattern, an ever-larger team falling further behind, has repeated at nearly every major financial institution in the last decade.

Compliance is the canonical schlep work: bureaucratic, paper-based, judgement-heavy, and historically a graveyard for startups. So when we say it's now one of the most engaging engineering problems in regulated industry, we mean something specific. Two thresholds have flipped at once, and the consequences for whoever builds the next generation of regulated software are large.

Threshold 1: the model finally clears the bar

In compliance the relevant accuracy bar is not "good enough to demo." It's good enough to sign. A KYC system that gets 90% of business onboardings right is not a 90% success and a 10% inconvenience. It is a 100% legal exposure. Every customer the model gets wrong is a regulator finding waiting to happen.

That asymmetry has held a generation of compliance AI back. OCR works "mostly." Rule engines fire "mostly." Mostly does not survive an audit. The shift in the last eighteen months has been less about a single capability and more about a stack of capabilities reaching the audit-safe threshold together:

  • Vision-language models that read a 400-page filing the way an analyst reads it. Not OCR with a thesaurus, actual document understanding, including layout, tables, signatures, and the relationship between them.
  • Computer-use agents that drive the legacy software a compliance officer drives. No six-month API integration with a 1990s core banking system. The agent uses the screen.
  • Long-horizon task execution: not "help me draft this paragraph" but "pull the transaction history, cross-check three databases, flag the anomalies, file the SAR." Multi-step, multi-tool, with state.
  • Legal-grade reasoning. Top models now score in the 80-100% range on LegalBench's reasoning tasks. Compliance is essentially applied legal reasoning under operational constraints — read the rule, apply it to a fact pattern, flag ambiguity.

No single one of these gets a product through compliance review. Together they do. The technology stopped being good enough to pilot and became good enough to trust.

Threshold 2: the buyer finally leans in

The second flip is on the buyer side, and it matters at least as much.

For thirty years, compliance functions had a single dominant posture: change is the riskier option. Migrations break audits. Audits break careers. So GRC tools written in 2004 kept running in 2024, stitched together by a human integration layer that copy-pasted fields between screens.

Two things have broken that equilibrium. First, compliance is no longer purely a cost center. Faster KYC is faster onboarding is faster revenue. Better AML monitoring is fewer false positives is fewer customer relationships destroyed. The competitive argument is now the cost of not modernizing is the business one of your competitors will take from you.

Second, agents are about to become buyers. Identity verification, intent inference, and liability allocation were all designed around human counterparties. When the party on the other side of a transaction is autonomous, the regulatory primitives have to be rewritten. There isn't a way to defer that conversation; the agents are already here.

A function that historically did not buy software has started leaning in. Not because it wants to. Because it has to.

Where AI lands: three wedges

Strip a compliance function down to its primitives and you find the same three ingredients everywhere: regulation (the rules and policies), software (the GRC platforms, case tools, screening engines), and people (the analysts moving data between the first two). Almost every compliance task is some combination of: extract information from a document, manually review that information for inconsistencies, and repeat on a cadence.

Take a Suspicious Activity Report. A transaction monitoring system fires an alert. Sarah, the analyst, opens the case, pivots to the core banking system to pull transaction history, pivots again to a different database for the KYC file, opens a shared drive for onboarding documents, checks the internal policy guideline, makes a judgement call, then returns to the alert tool and writes a narrative by hand, copying fields from each of the systems she just visited.

That sequence has three natural wedges for an AI product. We've shipped against all three. They look very different in code.

Wedge 1: turn regulation into code

Every new entry in Title 12, FINRA, the SEC, the CFTC, and the fifty state-level variants lands as a PDF that a person has to read, interpret, translate into internal policy, and then watch for changes. This is the most leveraged thing AI does in compliance: it parses regulation into a structured, machine-checkable set of obligations.

Two things change the moment regulation becomes code. Monitoring shifts from periodic to continuous; a rule that used to be sampled quarterly can be checked on every transaction. And rule changes propagate through an enterprise in minutes instead of quarters. The compliance officer in Brazilian payroll whose entire job is refreshing government sites for union-rule changes stops being a refresh button.

The engineering cost is in the trust loop. Parsing a regulation is not enough; you also have to keep an auditable chain of custody from the original text to the rule that fired to the decision the rule produced. That chain is what makes the system defensible in front of a regulator, and it's what most early attempts at "AI for compliance" have skipped.

Wedge 2: replace the system of record

The deeper problem under most regulated enterprises is that the system of record predates the cloud. Core banking, mortgage servicing, employee oversight — these ran on platforms designed for batch jobs and human operators. The integration layer between them is a person. That's the part you cannot AI your way around without replacing the floor.

Buyers now have three real options, and each has its own cost curve:

  1. Stay headless. Keep the legacy core, build agents and new interfaces on top. Lowest migration risk. Hard ceiling on what you can automate, because the underlying system was never designed to be read or written by software at speed.
  2. Rebuild yourself. Vibe-code a replacement system of record. Data model, permissions, workflows, integrations, auditability. This is tractable now in ways it wasn't five years ago, but it is still the most expensive option to do correctly.
  3. Buy an AI-native replacement. Move onto a system built from day one for machine readability, agent orchestration, and continuous auditability. Highest near-term migration cost, lowest long-term cost.

The instinct in any risk-averse function is option (1). The math of compounding AI advantage at competitors eventually forces (2) or (3). Legacy systems were built for humans: data is siloed, rules are hardcoded, workflows run in batches. You can wrap an agent around that. You cannot make it fast.

Wedge 3: augment the people

The third wedge is the one most teams reach for first, and the one with the cleanest near-term ROI. Don't replace the system. Don't rewrite the regulation. Drop an agent in next to the analyst.

Business banking onboarding is the cleanest example. The customer uploads incorporation documents, IDs, financial statements. Today, an analyst extracts the fields by hand, types them into three internal tools, and runs sanctions and registry checks one at a time. With a competent agent the documents are parsed instantly, every database is queried in parallel, and what reaches the human is the exception — the one onboarding where something didn't match — not the ninety-eight that did.

That shape — agent does the rote work, human reviews the exceptions — is the dominant pattern we see in every regulated deployment we've shipped. It also has the easiest narrative for the Chief Risk Officer: no system of record changes, no regulation is being interpreted by software unilaterally, the human is still on the decision.

What actually makes these systems shippable

The wedge you pick determines the demo. The engineering underneath determines whether the system survives an audit twelve months in. A few principles we keep re-learning:

  • Auditability is a first-class feature. Every decision the agent takes needs a trail: which rule fired, which version of the rule, which document produced which field, which model was on the other end. If a regulator asks "why did this account get approved on March 14," you have eight minutes to answer, not eight weeks.
  • Evaluation is the product. A compliance system without continuous evaluation is not a compliance system. Labelled sets curated from real production traffic, offline metrics on every model and prompt change, online metrics tied to actual regulatory outcomes (SAR acceptance rate, false-positive rate, exam findings).
  • Determinism where it matters. Use the model to choose the strategy. Use deterministic code to execute the strategy. A model decides whether a transaction looks suspicious; a deterministic pipeline writes the SAR. The non-determinism stays in the policy layer, not the execution layer.
  • Human-in-the-loop is a graph, not a checkbox. Different decisions need different review depths. Onboarding a new customer in a low-risk jurisdiction is one signoff. Filing a SAR is another. The system has to know which is which and route accordingly.
  • Plan for the long tail. Compliance is mostly the middle of the distribution, and entirely the tails. The agent that handles the 80% easy cases well and fails ungracefully on the 20% hard ones has made the analyst's life worse, not better. Design for the exceptions from day one.

The convergence

The interesting thing about the three wedges is that they converge. The team that wins a regulated category eventually does all three. They turn the regulation into code, because that's how the rules stay current. They own the system of record, because that's where the data and the audit trail live. They run a fleet of agents on top, because that's how the work actually gets done at speed.

The companies that look like piecemeal automation today will look like full-stack regulated platforms in three years. The compliance function inside those platforms will look almost nothing like a 2024 compliance function: smaller, sharper, focused on the exceptions and the policy work, with the analyst acting more like a senior reviewer than a data entry clerk.

For the first time in the history of regulated software, the question is not whether the technology is good enough. It's whether the engineering team is disciplined enough to ship it correctly.

That last part is the part nobody is talking about loudly enough. The hard work in regulated AI is not the model. The model is finally fine. The hard work is the audit trails, the evaluation harnesses, the deterministic execution layers, the human review graphs, and the slow patient labour of making a system that a regulator will sign off on without a fight.

Everything, everywhere, is becoming compliance. Compliance, in turn, is becoming engineering. That is good news for the engineers.
